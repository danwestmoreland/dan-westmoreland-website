The two-credit account brief.

Account research costs the same whether the account turns out to be a fit or not, which is why Clay bills surprise people. This play runs a cheap yes/no question on every account, a cheap second check on the maybes, and the expensive five-bullet brief only on what passes, with a hard credit cap per account so the run cannot blow up while you sleep. Same briefs, a quarter of the credits.

For: teams already paying for Clay who research 200 or more accounts a monthStack: Clay Workflows with per-workflow credit caps, Claygent on Auto-mode, HubSpot free, SmartleadCost: Clay Starter $134/month plus Smartlead $39Build time: half a day, plus a week of tuning the question

The play

The way most teams use Claygent is one prompt per account that asks for everything: what they do, what they run, who runs it, what changed, and a reason to write. Then they run it on the whole list. Half the list was never going to qualify, and you paid full price to find out. The invoice at the end of the month is the first time anyone notices.

Two things Clay shipped this fall make the fix cheap. Claygent Auto-mode (beta, October 27) picks a model per row and optimizes for cost, so a yes/no question runs on a small model when it can. Per-workflow credit caps (in the November 2 roundup) let you tell a Workflow the most it may spend, and it stops when it hits the number instead of finishing the batch and billing you.

So the research becomes a funnel instead of a flat fee. Stage one asks one question that disqualifies: for Ironvale in the mock, "does this company take purchase requests by email or spreadsheet?" No means stop. Unknown means a cheap tech-stack lookup, and if a procurement suite turns up, stop. Only a yes gets the full brief, five bullets, each with a source, and the first line of the email quotes bullet one. A cap of two credits per account sits over the whole thing, so the worst case is a logged row that says "capped," not a bill.

Who it's for

  • You pay for Clay and your Claygent spend is the line item you can't explain. If you're on the free plan's 100 credits, this is how you make them last.
  • Your ICP has one disqualifying fact that is usually visible on a careers page, a supplier page, or a blog. The whole play is finding that one question.
  • You send researched first lines, not templates, and you want to keep doing that at 500 accounts instead of 50.
  • Somebody looks at the capped rows once a week. The cap is a safety rail, not a decision.

  1. Find the one question. It must disqualify, it must be answerable from a public page, and it must come back as yes, no, or unknown with a URL. "Do they take purchase requests by email or spreadsheet?" works for Ironvale because careers pages and supplier pages say so. Spend a day on this and the rest is plumbing.
  2. Set the cap before anything runs. Two credits per account in the mock. Set it where one cheap question plus one cheap lookup plus one brief fits, and nothing else does. The cap is the budget, not a target.
  3. Stage one on Auto-mode. One Claygent column, the question above, the instruction to answer in one word plus a URL, and nothing else. Auto-mode will pick a small model for most rows.
  4. Branch on the answer. No: suppress and log the reason with the URL. Unknown: tech-stack lookup. Yes: straight to the brief. The branch is the funnel; it's three paths in the Workflow canvas.
  5. Check the maybes cheaply. The tech lookup asks one thing: is there a procurement suite installed? Coupa, Ariba, Jaggaer, Ivalua, Zip, Procurify, GEP. Any hit means they have a process and you stop. Nothing found means continue.
  6. Brief only what's left. Five bullets, each with a source URL, on a stronger model. The first bullet must be the disqualifying fact itself, because that's the one the email will quote. No URL, no bullet.
  7. Write the first line from bullet one. Strip the URL, quote the fact, add one sentence about why you're early. It reads like a person noticed something, because one did; the person just happened to be a workflow.
  8. Read the capped rows weekly. They're the accounts where a mid-size model answered "unknown" and the lookup didn't settle it. A human decides in ten minutes whether to pay for the brief. If more than a tenth of rows cap, the question is too hard for the small model; make it narrower.

What breaks

The small model says "unknown" to everything. Auto-mode is in beta, and a vague question gets a vague answer, which pushes every row through the lookup and the cap. If more than a tenth of your rows cap, the question is the problem. Narrow it until a small model can answer from one page.

The cap stops a run mid-batch. That is the point, but the partial rows need a status. "Capped" with the credits spent is enough; what you can't have is a row that looks unresearched when it cost 0.9.

The brief invents a bullet. Any research step will, some weeks, produce a confident sentence about nothing. The rule is a URL on every bullet, and the first line only ever quotes bullet one, which is the fact the small model already found a page for. A human spot-checks five briefs a week.

Your costs aren't these. The mock's step costs are made up to make the arithmetic readable. Clay prices research by model and by step, and it changes. Put your real per-step numbers in rules.csv before you believe the savings.

The stage-one question is a leading question. "Does this company have a messy purchasing process?" gets yes from any model. Ask about an observable fact with a yes/no answer, not a judgment about pain.

Copy it

Take the four files and run the script as-is to see the funnel. Then replace the question in rules.csv with your own disqualifier, set the step costs to what your Clay plan actually charges, and export 40 of your target accounts into accounts.csv. Build the three-path Workflow with the cap on, and run it on those 40 before you run it on 500. If you'd rather have someone look at your Clay spend first, that's what the Clay audit is.
